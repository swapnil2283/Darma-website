What is PRP?

Science of Hair Regeneration

PRP (Platelet-Rich Plasma) is derived from your own blood. After drawing a small sample, we spin it in a centrifuge to concentrate the platelets — tiny cells packed with growth factors that trigger tissue repair and regeneration.

When injected into the scalp at the level of the hair follicles, these growth factors wake dormant follicles, strengthen existing hair, improve scalp blood supply, and significantly reduce shedding — all without any synthetic chemicals.

01
Blood Draw
A small sample (15–20 ml) of your blood is drawn from your arm — similar to a routine blood test.
02
Centrifugation
Your blood is spun in our specialized centrifuge to separate and concentrate the platelets.
03
Scalp Preparation
The scalp is cleansed and a topical numbing cream is applied for a comfortable experience.
04
PRP Injection
The concentrated PRP is micro-injected at 1 cm intervals across areas of hair thinning or loss.

The Procedure

FUE Hair Transplant

Follicular Unit Extraction (FUE) is the gold standard in modern hair transplantation. Individual hair follicles are extracted from the donor area (typically the back and sides of the scalp) and transplanted to thinning or bald areas with artistic precision — matching your natural hair direction, angle, and density.

01
Design & Planning
Dr. Arjun Patel designs your new hairline, calculates graft requirements, and plans the extraction zone.
02
Donor Harvesting
Individual grafts are extracted one by one using a micro-punch tool under local anesthesia — no linear scar.
03
Graft Processing
Extracted follicles are sorted, counted, and preserved in a specialized solution to maximize survival rate.
04
Recipient Site Creation
Micro-channels are made in the recipient area following the natural hair growth pattern for undetectable results.
05
Graft Implantation
Each graft is carefully placed into the recipient sites with forceps, achieving natural density and coverage.
06
Recovery & Growth
Transplanted hairs shed at 2–6 weeks (normal), then regrow permanently from month 3 onwards.

Skincare Science

The Science Behind Vitamin C Serums:
What Actually Works

Home › Blog › Vitamin C Serums
Dr. Meera Sharma
MD Dermatology · Lead Dermatologist, DermaVeda
May 2, 20255 min read

Walk into any pharmacy or skincare aisle and you'll find dozens of Vitamin C serums promising brighter skin, reduced pigmentation, and a youthful glow. But as a dermatologist, I can tell you that most of them don't work — not because the ingredient is ineffective, but because the formulation is wrong.

Vitamin C (L-ascorbic acid) is one of the most researched skincare actives in dermatology. The evidence for its benefits is robust: it's a powerful antioxidant, it inhibits melanin synthesis (reducing pigmentation), it stimulates collagen production, and it provides meaningful UV protection when combined with sunscreen. The problem lies entirely in delivery.

The pH Problem

L-ascorbic acid is only effective at a skin pH below 3.5. At higher pH levels, the molecule cannot penetrate the skin barrier and is essentially useless — sitting on the surface until it oxidizes.

Most commercially available vitamin C serums are formulated at pH 5–7 because they're more stable, feel better on skin, and cause less irritation. They're also ineffective. If a vitamin C serum feels completely comfortable with no tingling whatsoever on first application, it's almost certainly too high in pH to work.

"If a vitamin C serum feels completely comfortable with zero tingling on first use, it's likely too high in pH to penetrate effectively."

Concentration Matters — But There's a Ceiling

Research shows that L-ascorbic acid becomes maximally effective at concentrations between 10–20%. Below 10%, you may see antioxidant benefits but minimal pigmentation or collagen effect. Above 20%, skin irritation increases significantly without proportional benefit — there's a ceiling to how much the skin can absorb.

Look for serums in the 15% range for a balance of efficacy and tolerability.

Derivative Forms: Are They Better?

Vitamin C derivatives like sodium ascorbyl phosphate, ascorbyl glucoside, and magnesium ascorbyl phosphate are more stable and gentler — but they must be converted to L-ascorbic acid by skin enzymes to work. This conversion is incomplete and variable, making them generally less potent than well-formulated L-ascorbic acid.

That said, for sensitive skin types (including many Indian skin tones prone to post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation), derivatives can be a smarter choice with consistent use.

The Oxidation Problem

Vitamin C oxidizes rapidly when exposed to light, air, and heat. An oxidized vitamin C serum is not only ineffective — it can actually cause yellow-brown discoloration on the skin. If your serum has turned orange or brown in the bottle, it's time to replace it.

What to look for:

  • Dark, airtight packaging (amber glass or airless pump)
  • Small batch sizes you'll use within 1–2 months
  • Ferulic acid in the formula (stabilizes Vitamin C + doubles photoprotection)
  • Vitamin E as a synergistic antioxidant partner
  • Storage away from heat and light

Our Recommendation

At DermaVeda, we recommend combining a professionally formulated vitamin C serum (pH 2.5–3.5, 15% L-ascorbic acid, with ferulic acid) with our clinical Vitamin C brightening infusion for optimal pigmentation correction in Indian skin.

If you're unsure which formulation is right for your skin type and concerns, book a skin analysis consultation — we'll guide you to what will actually work for your specific skin, not just what's trending.

Hair Care

PRP vs. Minoxidil:
Choosing the Right Treatment

Home › Blog › PRP vs Minoxidil
Dr. Arjun Patel
Senior Trichologist, DermaVeda
April 18, 20257 min read

Hair loss affects over 50% of men and 25% of women by age 50, making it one of the most common concerns I see in my clinic every day. Two treatments dominate the conversation: Minoxidil — the over-the-counter topical medication — and PRP (Platelet-Rich Plasma) therapy, an in-clinic procedure using your own blood's growth factors.

Both work. Both have solid clinical evidence. But they work differently, and the right choice depends entirely on your type of hair loss, stage, lifestyle, and goals.

How Minoxidil Works

Minoxidil was originally developed as an oral blood pressure medication. The discovery that it caused hair growth was a serendipitous side effect. It works primarily by extending the anagen (growth) phase of the hair cycle and widening blood vessels around the follicle — improving nutrient delivery.

It's available as a 2% or 5% topical solution or foam, applied once or twice daily. The results are real but require continuous use: stop Minoxidil, and the hair gained typically falls out within 3–6 months.

"Minoxidil requires lifelong commitment. PRP stimulates your follicles' own biology — the results are more durable."

How PRP Works

PRP contains concentrated growth factors including PDGF, TGF-β, VEGF, and IGF — all of which play active roles in follicle stimulation and hair cycle regulation. When injected into the scalp, these growth factors activate dormant follicles, reduce inflammation, and strengthen existing hair from root to tip.

Unlike Minoxidil, PRP doesn't require daily application. A course of 3–6 sessions is followed by maintenance sessions every 6–12 months — making compliance much easier for patients who struggle with daily topical routines.

Side-by-Side Comparison

  • Minoxidil: Daily application required · OTC availability · Cheaper per month · Shedding on cessation
  • PRP: 3–6 clinic sessions · Prescription not needed · Higher upfront cost · More durable results
  • Best combined: For moderate-to-severe hair loss, combining both yields superior results to either alone

Who Should Choose What

Choose Minoxidil if: you're in the early stages of hair loss, you're comfortable with a daily routine, or you need a low-cost starting point while awaiting consultation.

Choose PRP if: you've tried Minoxidil with partial results, you want a more natural approach, your hair loss is moderate, or you're a poor Minoxidil complier. PRP is also excellent as a post-hair-transplant accelerant.

Consider combining both if: your hair loss is significant and you want maximum results in the shortest possible time.

The most important step is getting a proper trichoscopy evaluation first — to determine whether your follicles are still viable, which directly affects whether any medical treatment will work.

Anti-Aging

Botox at 35:
Is Preventive Treatment Right for You?

Home › Blog › Botox at 35
Dr. Meera Sharma
MD Dermatology · Lead Dermatologist
April 5, 20256 min read

The age of Botox has shifted significantly over the past decade. When I began my dermatology practice in 2012, the average patient seeking Botox was in their late 40s or early 50s, looking to soften well-established lines. Today, I regularly see patients in their late 20s and 30s asking about "preventive" Botox — and many of them are asking the right question.

What Is Preventive Botox?

Preventive (or "Baby") Botox refers to the use of small doses of botulinum toxin to relax facial muscles before deep static lines form. The logic is elegant: wrinkles form because muscles repeatedly contract over decades, eventually creasing the overlying skin permanently. If you reduce those contractions earlier, you prevent those permanent creases from forming in the first place.

The science supports this. A 2018 study following identical twins (one treated with Botox, one not) over 13 years found that the treated twin had significantly fewer and shallower wrinkles by the study's end.

"Prevention is always easier than correction. In anti-aging dermatology, starting earlier means needing less treatment later."

Who Is a Good Candidate at 35?

Not everyone at 35 needs Botox — and a good dermatologist will tell you honestly if you don't. Signs that preventive Botox might be right for you:

  • You're seeing early expression lines at rest (lines visible even when your face is relaxed)
  • You have strong muscle activity in the forehead or between the brows
  • You have a family history of early deep forehead or glabellar lines
  • You squint frequently due to sun exposure or screen work
  • You've maximized sun protection and skincare and want an additional tool

What to Expect from Baby Botox

At a preventive dose, the goal is not to eliminate all movement — it's to soften it. You should still be able to raise your eyebrows, show surprise, and express emotion naturally. The aim is a 30–50% reduction in muscle movement in targeted areas, not paralysis.

Treatment takes 15–20 minutes. Results appear in 3–5 days and last 4–6 months. Because preventive doses are lower, touch-ups may be needed slightly more frequently than therapeutic doses in older patients.

Is It Safe Long-Term?

Botulinum toxin has been used in medicine for over 40 years and in cosmetic dermatology for over 25 years. Its safety profile is excellent when administered by a qualified physician. The concern about muscle atrophy with long-term use is real but rarely clinically significant at cosmetic doses.

My recommendation: start conservative, maintain realistic expectations, and work with a doctor who will say "you don't need it yet" when that's the honest answer.
